Climbing Stairs

Bottom-up DP ⏱ O(n) time 💾 O(n) space

Problem

You are climbing a staircase with n steps. Each move you climb 1 or 2 steps. How many distinct ways can you reach the top?

Input:  n = 3
Output: 3  (1+1+1, 1+2, 2+1)

Intuition

Think about the last move that lands you on step i: it was either a 1-step hop from i−1 or a 2-step hop from i−2. Those are the only options, and they never overlap.

So the number of ways to reach step i is exactly dp[i−1] + dp[i−2] — the Fibonacci recurrence in disguise. Solve the tiny cases by hand (dp[1] = 1, dp[2] = 2), then each new answer is one addition using answers we already computed. That's the whole essence of dynamic programming: solve small, reuse, build up.

Full Explanation

The key question in any dynamic programming problem is: what does the answer to a big case look like in terms of smaller cases? Here, every way of reaching step i ends with either a single step from i minus one, or a double step from i minus two. Since those two groups can't overlap and cover every possibility, the count for step i is simply the sum of the counts for the two steps below it.

We anchor the recurrence with the trivial cases — one way to reach step one, two ways to reach step two — and then fill the table upward, each entry costing one addition. The final entry is the answer. This is the Fibonacci sequence wearing a staircase costume, and because each entry only ever looks back two slots, the table can even be replaced by two rolling variables for constant space.
