House Robber

Decision DP ⏱ O(n) time 💾 O(n) space

Problem

Each house on a street holds nums[i] money. You can't rob two adjacent houses (alarms connect neighbors). Return the maximum you can rob.

Input:  nums = [2,7,9,3,1]
Output: 12  (rob houses 0, 2, 4 → 2 + 9 + 1)

Intuition

Standing at house i, you face one binary decision: rob it or skip it. Skip it, and the best you can do is whatever was best up to house i−1. Rob it, and you're locked out of i−1 — you get nums[i] plus the best up to i−2.

So dp[i] = max(dp[i−1], dp[i−2] + nums[i]). Every entry is one comparison over answers we already have. Even better: the finished table remembers why each choice won — walking it backwards reveals exactly which houses to rob, not just the total.

Full Explanation

Define dp of i as the most money obtainable considering only the first i plus one houses. At each house the robber makes one choice. Skipping house i means the total stays at dp of i minus one. Robbing house i adds its value to dp of i minus two, because the adjacent house is now off limits. The larger of those two candidates becomes dp of i.

The base cases anchor everything: with one house you rob it, and with two you rob the richer one. After filling the table left to right, the last entry is the maximum loot. To recover the actual plan, walk the table backwards: if dp of i equals dp of i minus one, house i was skipped, so step back one; otherwise house i was robbed, so collect it and jump back two. This traceback costs nothing extra and turns an abstract number into a concrete heist plan — a technique that works for almost any dynamic program.
